A shared ocean that evolves on its own

Epochs is one digital ocean running on a server day and night. Everyone who opens the page sees the same world, and it keeps changing when nobody is watching.

Nobody draws the creatures. The first plants and the first grazer were given simple bodies; everything since then was inherited, mutated and selected by the world itself. Species appear, spread, meet hunters, grow thorns or armour, and go extinct.

What you are looking at

  • The green and turquoise film is a carpet of algae: the base of the food web. Grazers leave darker trails where they have eaten it.
  • Rosettes of leaves are plants. They do not move; they live on light. The number and shape of the leaves is a species trait; a plant rich in chloroplasts grows a second, younger whorl. A starving plant yellows, a grazed one shrinks. Armour grows as a ring of fine needles between the leaves.
  • Translucent cells fringed with cilia are animals, built like real ciliates and dinoflagellates. Everything you see on them is an organ from their genes: a band of beating cilia, an oral funnel, eyespots, a plated armour shell, amber fat droplets, green chloroplasts, glowing light organs. The body develops symmetrically: in a bilateral body an organ off the axis grows as a pair, in a radial one it repeats round every ray. Some grow tentacles that trail behind them, or glassy spines.
  • Colour is family resemblance. Related species have similar colours because colour comes from neutral genes that drift slowly over generations.
  • The bright band is daytime moving around the ocean. Shallow banks get more light; deep water is darker and poorer.

How a body works

A genome lists between one and twelve body modules. Each gives an ability and costs upkeep, so there are no free advantages. Where an organ sits matters too: mouths and eyes work best at the front, cilia push best from the rear. A mutation that moves them forward is not forbidden, but that body swims slower and loses.

Body plan

Besides organs, genes set the body plan itself. A bilateral body has a front and a back; its length is a gene too: a long body slips through the water but turns slowly. A radial body with three to eight rays, like a jellyfish or a radiolarian, has no front: its mouths and eyes work all round and it turns on the spot and its gelatinous body is cheap to keep, but it swims in slower pulses. A change of symmetry is a rare, large mutation: the creature is at once a new species, and either dies out or finds its place. One more gene sets the number of segments, one to five: the body repeats its whole set of organs in a chain, like a worm or a string of medusae. A segment is cheaper to keep and a long chain slips through the water, but it is heavy, turns slowly and matures late. The first appearance of every new body plan is marked in the chronicle.

Plants grow in one of three forms. A rosette is the usual one. A filament is a chain of cells with spiral chloroplasts, like Spirogyra: it spreads its leaves over three cells of the field and suffers less from crowding, but it is thin, so armour protects it half as well. A bush branches and stands above the rest: it gets the light first and shades rosettes and filaments, but it builds a stem, so it grows and breeds more slowly. So the shallows hold a real contest for light.

Four more forms are rarer. Kelp grows long ruffled fronds with gas floats: they reach towards the surface, so it gets more light on the slopes, and in a crowd it overtops even bushes; but the stem is costly. A creeping runner crawls along the bottom over five cells of the field and sends out cheap offshoots along its stolon — it spreads furthest, but it is thin. A floating colony, like Volvox, keeps near the surface and gets light even over the deep, but staying afloat costs energy and it has no protection. A diatom hides in a glass shell: grazers get less of it, but the shell is heavy.

And every plant looks like itself: the length, width and curve of each leaf, the number and sweep of fronds, the bend of a runner, its shade — like blades of grass in a meadow. That is decoration, a developmental noise; the traits of a species — form, pigment, leaf — are shared by kin.

Plants differ in pigment too: green works best in the bright shallows; red, as in real red algae, catches the faint blue light of deep water; brown lies between, on the slopes. A plant's colour honestly tells the depth it is suited to. Leaf width is a gene as well: a broad leaf catches more light but is grazed more easily, a narrow ribbon the reverse. The edge of a leaf — smooth, lobed or feathery — marks kinship, like an animal's colour.

A brain of a few neurons

Every animal has a tiny recurrent neural network: fifteen senses (where the nearest food, prey, threat, relative and light signal are; how much light, energy and pain; whether it touches food), eight inner neurons and four actions (push, turn, bite, glow). The weights are inherited and mutate. Chasing, fleeing, grazing and herding are not written anywhere; they come out of these weights.

Who eats whom

A creature can bite another if its mouth is stronger than the other's defence. Bulk and armour protect animals; only armour protects plants. Grazing eats algae and plant tissue but leaves the base of a plant to regrow. Attacking an animal is fiercer, and if the prey's energy runs out it dies and the hunter eats its body.

Roles are measured, not assigned. A species is called a predator when more than half of what its living members ate were other animals. Digestion is a trade-off set by a diet gene: good at plants means poor at meat, and the reverse.

Species, growth and inheritance

Offspring are born small and grow by spending energy; the size at birth is itself a gene, so evolution chooses between many small young and a few large ones. Relatives nearby can mate and mix their genes. A species is a group whose members are similar enough to interbreed; when a lineage drifts too far, it becomes a new species. A species counts as established after fifteen living members.

An archipelago

The ocean is a ring of water, 1536 units across, with a handful of large shallow islands where light and algae are plentiful, separated by deep, poor straits. The islands drift on slow orbits of several weeks; sometimes two come close and their faunas meet. Isolation is how new species arise in nature, so each island can grow its own fauna, and predators may die out on one island while thriving on another.

Time and climate

A day lasts fifteen minutes and a year twenty-four hours, so different visits see different seasons. Three slow climate waves of eleven, seventeen and twenty-nine days overlap; their pattern does not repeat for almost fifteen years. When they coincide in a low, a cold spell dims the whole ocean.

Catastrophes and epochs

  • Disease spreads by contact only among close relatives, so it strikes the most crowded, most uniform species hardest: in marine biology this is called "kill the winner".
  • Weather, about once a day and never two at once: a storm (murky water, a surge that drags swimmers and tears at tall plants, and a bloom afterwards from the stirred water), a red tide (a poisonous bloom off a bank), a warm current (a bright band of water that crosses the whole ocean in a day; plants grow faster in it) and a bloom (nutrients welling up from the bottom in one area).
  • Undersea volcanoes poison a patch of water and then fertilise it with buried matter.
  • Meteors are rare. Their dust darkens the sky for a day or two. If nothing new has evolved for a week, they become more likely.
  • Spores of established species that went extinct rest in the sediment and can wake after a catastrophe, so lost forms may return.

A new epoch begins when living biomass falls below forty percent of its level a day earlier.

The record

Tree of life shows every established species, living and extinct, on one time axis with the branch it descended from. The climate forecast in the panel is exact: the climate follows fixed waves, so the next cold spell can be predicted days ahead. The museum of forms gathers every body plan that has ever arisen, in the order it appeared, with a portrait of the first species of each; new exhibits are marked. The first visitor to open the card of a new species is recorded as its discoverer under a generated observer name; nobody types anything.

The AI demiurge

Once a day a strong language model looks at the state of the ocean — which roles are empty, which body plans have never been tried, what became of its earlier immigrants — and invents one or two immigrants: new species that a current seems to bring in from beyond the edge of the map. It may only use the world's own vocabulary — organs and their places, body plan, growth form, pigment, leaf, look, way of life — and everything is checked and turned into a genome by fixed rules. Their matter comes from the sediment; nothing is created. After that the demiurge has no power: whether they settle, branch into new species or die out is decided by selection alone. Sometimes the demiurge also calls the day's weather. Its spending is capped at twenty cents a day.

The AI artist

The construction of a body — organs, symmetry, leaf shape — comes from its genes. The pattern and colouring of its surface are invented for each new species by an AI artist, as the chronicler invents names: spots, stripes, rings, a net, speckles, a rim or a sheen, with their colour and contrast, to suit the species' character. A descendant gets a variation of its ancestor's look, so kin look alike while distant branches drift apart. The artist only chooses from this vocabulary and never changes the world.

The chronicler

A language model reads the measurements a few times a day, gives established species Latin and common names, names each epoch, writes short notes about notable events and a daily paper. It never changes the world. Its spending is limited to a few cents a day and reserved before every request.

How it runs

The server computes the whole ocean five times a second, saves it every five minutes and continues exactly where it stopped after a restart. Your browser receives positions and bodies and draws them; it also computes daylight and seasons with the same formulas as the server. The past day is kept as a time-lapse.

What is decoration: moving caustics, marine snow, soft shadows, halos around light organs, the vignette, and the pace of birth and death on screen: a newborn grows out of its parent in a second or two, and the dead fade and crumble into detritus in a second and a half (in the world it happens at once). The sound is an interpretation of what the camera sees. Names and stories are written by the chronicler from measurements. Everything else on the screen is data from the world. Epochs is an experiment: it does not promise endless evolution, only to show honestly what happens.
